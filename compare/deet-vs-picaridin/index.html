DEET vs picaridin

Two repellents that both do the job. The differences are practical, not about safety.

← Back
Quick answerAt 20%, picaridin matches 20–30% DEET against mosquitoes and is at least as good against ticks, lasts as long or longer, has no smell, doesn't dissolve plastics or synthetics, and is less likely to irritate skin. DEET's advantages are its six-decade safety record, wider availability, and slightly more data in pregnancy. For most people — and especially for children, gear-heavy activities, and anyone who dislikes the DEET feel — picaridin 20% is the better everyday choice. Either is far better than untested alternatives.

Effectiveness

Head-to-head trials consistently show 20% picaridin and 20–30% DEET providing similar protection against mosquitoes, typically 8 hours or more. Against ticks, a 2015 European study found icaridin at least as effective as DEET, and some field work in the US has favoured picaridin against the blacklegged tick. Lower picaridin concentrations (5–10%) are noticeably weaker and shorter-lived, which is where its reputation for being 'less effective' comes from — the concentration, not the molecule.

Both work on biting flies, chiggers, and fleas. Neither is a good choice against bed bugs or head lice.

Safety

Neither is carcinogenic, a hormone disruptor, or teratogenic at label use. DEET's rare neurological case reports almost all involve ingestion or extreme misuse; picaridin has no such reports, though it's had a third of the time to accumulate them. Both irritate eyes. Picaridin causes less skin irritation and is the one dermatologists tend to suggest for sensitive skin.

In pregnancy both are recommended by the CDC. DEET has the stronger data — a randomised trial in 897 pregnant women found no adverse effects. Picaridin's data are more limited but show nothing concerning.

Practical differences that decide it

  • If you wear a watch, sunglasses, or synthetic sports fabric — picaridin. DEET will cloud or soften them.
  • If you're spraying kids who'll touch their face — picaridin's lack of odour and lower irritation make compliance easier.
  • If you're travelling somewhere with malaria or dengue and want the maximum evidence base — either, with DEET having the edge on data.
  • If you have very sensitive skin — picaridin.
  • If cost matters and you're covering a large group — DEET is cheaper per application.
  • For ticks on a long hike — either, plus permethrin-treated clothing, which matters more than the choice between them.

What about the alternatives?

Oil of lemon eucalyptus (PMD) at 30% is a legitimate third option with 4–6 hours of protection, but not for children under three. IR3535 is gentle but short-lived. Everything else sold as a 'natural' repellent — citronella, lavender, essential-oil blends, wristbands — provides little or no protection in controlled tests. Choosing one of those over DEET or picaridin in tick or mosquito-disease country is the risky choice.

Common questions

Is picaridin safer than DEET?

Both are safe as directed. Picaridin irritates skin less and has no neurological case reports; DEET has a longer track record. The differences are small enough that convenience and feel are reasonable grounds for choosing.

Which lasts longer?

At 20%, picaridin typically lasts 8–14 hours against mosquitoes; DEET at 20–30% lasts 4–8. Higher DEET concentrations extend that but don't improve repellency.

Can I use both?

No need. Choose one for skin, and add permethrin on clothing if ticks are the concern.

Which is better for children?

Both are approved from 2 months in the US. Many paediatricians now suggest picaridin 20% first for its lower irritation and lack of smell, with DEET 10–30% as an equally valid alternative.
