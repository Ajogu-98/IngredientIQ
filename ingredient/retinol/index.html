Retinol

The gold standard for wrinkles and acne, with two real caveats: don't use it while pregnant, and expect your skin to protest at first.

← Back !Use With Caution
Quick answerRetinol (vitamin A) is safe and effective for most adults when introduced gradually, and it's one of the few cosmetic ingredients with strong evidence behind its anti-ageing claims. The exceptions matter: all retinoids are advised against in pregnancy and while trying to conceive because of the link between vitamin A derivatives and birth defects, and the EU has set new maximum concentrations (0.3% in most products, 0.05% in body lotion) that apply to products sold from November 2025. Irritation, peeling, and sun sensitivity are normal in the first weeks.

What is Retinol?

Retinol is a form of vitamin A. Once in the skin, enzymes convert it in two steps to retinoic acid, the molecule that actually does the work. That conversion is why over-the-counter retinol is gentler and slower than prescription tretinoin, which is retinoic acid itself.

The retinoid family on cosmetic labels runs from weakest to strongest: retinyl palmitate and retinyl acetate (esters, mild), retinol, retinaldehyde (one step from retinoic acid), and the prescription-only retinoic acid. Adapalene is a synthetic retinoid available over the counter for acne in some countries.

What does it do in a product?

Retinoids speed up skin-cell turnover, increase collagen production, and normalise how skin cells mature and shed. In practice that means finer lines, more even tone, smaller-looking pores, and fewer clogged pores and breakouts. The evidence for these effects is among the strongest in dermatology; tretinoin has decades of clinical trials behind it and retinol has consistent, if smaller, data.

Results take time — typically 8 to 12 weeks for visible change and 6 months or more for the full effect on wrinkles.

Is Retinol safe?

For non-pregnant adults, the main issue is irritation. 'Retinisation' — redness, dryness, flaking, and stinging in the first 2 to 6 weeks — is expected and usually resolves as skin adapts. Starting with a low concentration two or three nights a week and building up prevents most of it. Retinoids also make skin more sensitive to UV, so daily sunscreen is not optional.

The pregnancy concern is real and well established. High-dose oral vitamin A and the oral retinoid isotretinoin are potent teratogens (causes of birth defects). Topical retinol is absorbed in far smaller amounts, and no clear harm from it has been demonstrated — but because the potential consequence is severe and the benefit is cosmetic, virtually all dermatology and obstetric bodies advise stopping all retinoids when pregnant, breastfeeding, or trying to conceive.

The EU's 2024 rule change reflects a different worry: total vitamin A intake. Europeans already get substantial vitamin A from food and supplements, and the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety calculated that high-strength cosmetics could push some people over the safe upper limit. The new caps — 0.3% retinol equivalent in face and hand products, 0.05% in body lotion — apply to products placed on the market from 1 November 2025, with existing stock allowed until May 2027. Products must also carry a warning that they contain vitamin A.

Retinol is unstable in light and air. A product in a clear jar that's been open for months may have little active ingredient left.

Who should be careful

  • Anyone pregnant, breastfeeding, or trying to conceive — stop all retinoids
  • People using oral isotretinoin or prescription tretinoin (don't stack)
  • Skin with active eczema, rosacea flares, or a compromised barrier — wait until it's calm
  • Before waxing, laser, chemical peels, or heavy sun exposure — pause for a week
  • People who won't wear daily sunscreen

Where you'll find it

  • Night serums and creams labelled anti-ageing or 'renewal'
  • Acne treatments
  • Eye creams (usually low strength)
  • Body lotions marketed for texture or 'crepey' skin
  • Some hand creams

Alternatives

  • Bakuchiol — plant-derived, similar mechanism in early studies, generally considered safe in pregnancy though data are limited
  • Azelaic acid — pregnancy-safe option for acne and pigmentation
  • Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) — antioxidant and collagen support, pregnancy-safe
  • Peptides — gentler collagen signalling, weaker evidence
  • Glycolic or lactic acid — exfoliation without retinoid effects

Common questions

Can I use retinol while pregnant?

Standard medical advice is no — stop all retinoids while pregnant, breastfeeding, or trying to conceive. Topical retinol hasn't been proven to cause harm, but oral vitamin A derivatives cause serious birth defects and the cosmetic benefit doesn't justify the uncertainty. Azelaic acid, vitamin C, and bakuchiol are the usual substitutes.

What strength should I start with?

0.1% to 0.3% two or three nights a week is the standard starting point. Increase frequency first, then strength, over a couple of months. Higher isn't better if your skin can't tolerate it consistently.

Why is the EU limiting retinol?

Not because topical retinol is dangerous on its own, but because the EU calculated that high-strength cosmetics on top of dietary and supplement vitamin A could push some people past the safe upper intake. The 0.3% limit still allows effective products.

Is retinyl palmitate the same as retinol?

It's a milder ester of retinol that the skin must convert further before it works. It's often used in daytime products and body lotions. It counts toward the EU retinol-equivalent limit.

Can I use retinol with vitamin C or acids?

Yes, but not all at once when starting out. A common approach is vitamin C in the morning and retinol at night, and skipping exfoliating acids on retinol nights until your skin has adapted.
