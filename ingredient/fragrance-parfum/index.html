Fragrance (Parfum)

One word on the label, potentially a hundred ingredients behind it. The single most common cause of allergic reactions to cosmetics.

What is Fragrance (Parfum)?

In most countries, a manufacturer can list a scent blend as a single ingredient — 'fragrance' or 'parfum' — because the blend is treated as a trade secret. That one word can represent anywhere from a handful to over a hundred separate compounds: synthetic aroma chemicals, natural extracts and essential oils, fixatives that make the scent last, and solvents that carry it.

'Natural fragrance' and 'essential oils' are not automatically safer. Limonene, linalool, geraniol, and citral — all common in essential oils — are among the most frequent fragrance allergens.

What does it do in a product?

Fragrance makes a product smell pleasant or masks the smell of its raw materials, which is often the real reason it's there. It has no functional benefit for skin or hair. The exception is products like perfume, where the scent is the product.

Fixatives in the blend, historically including phthalates such as diethyl phthalate (DEP), keep the scent from evaporating quickly. DEP is still permitted in the EU and US, though many brands have dropped it.

Is Fragrance (Parfum) safe?

Fragrance is the number one cause of allergic contact dermatitis from cosmetics, ahead of preservatives. The typical picture is itchy, red, sometimes scaly patches where scented products are applied — face, neck, hands, armpits — or airborne reactions on the face and eyelids from scented sprays and cleaning products.

The EU addressed the disclosure problem in 2005 by requiring 26 specific fragrance allergens (limonene, linalool, citronellol, geraniol, eugenol, and others) to be listed by name when they exceed 0.001% in leave-on or 0.01% in rinse-off products. Regulation 2023/1545 expands that list to more than 80 substances, with new products required to comply from July 2026 and existing stock by 2028. This is why European labels often end with a string of chemical names that US labels lack.

In the United States, no disclosure is required at all on the label. California's SB 312 (2020) requires companies to report certain fragrance allergens and hazardous ingredients to the state, but that information doesn't appear on packaging.

Beyond allergy, fragrance ingredients are a common trigger for migraine and asthma in susceptible people, and scented products are routinely advised against for eczema, rosacea, and post-procedure skin.

Who should be careful

  • Anyone with eczema, rosacea, or chronically sensitive skin
  • People with a patch-test-confirmed fragrance allergy (fragrance mix I and II are in every standard test series)
  • Babies and young children — most paediatric guidance recommends fragrance-free products
  • Skin after a procedure, sunburn, or while using retinoids or acids
  • People with fragrance-triggered migraine or asthma, particularly with sprays and scented cleaners

Where you'll find it

  • Perfume, cologne, and body spray
  • Moisturisers, body lotion, and hand cream
  • Shampoo, conditioner, and body wash
  • Deodorant
  • Laundry detergent, fabric softener, and dryer sheets
  • Air fresheners, candles, and cleaning sprays

Alternatives

  • Products labelled 'fragrance-free' — no scent added at all
  • Look for the full allergen list on EU-labelled products and avoid the ones you react to
  • Avoid 'unscented', which usually contains a masking fragrance
  • If you love scent, apply perfume to clothing rather than skin

Common questions

What's the difference between fragrance-free and unscented?

'Fragrance-free' means no fragrance ingredients were added. 'Unscented' means the product has no noticeable smell, which often means a masking fragrance was added to cover the raw-material odour. If you're avoiding fragrance, choose fragrance-free.

Are natural fragrances or essential oils safer?

Not reliably. Many of the most common fragrance allergens (limonene, linalool, geraniol, eugenol) are natural components of essential oils. 'Natural' describes the source, not the allergy risk.

Does fragrance in products contain phthalates?

Sometimes. Diethyl phthalate (DEP) is used as a fixative and solvent and is still permitted. Because it's inside the 'fragrance' term you can't tell from a US label. EU and Canadian labels won't show it either unless the brand chooses to disclose. Brands that state 'phthalate-free' are the only reliable indicator.

Can I be allergic to fragrance if I've used perfume for years?

Yes. Fragrance allergy typically develops after years of exposure, and reactions to a long-used product are common. Once sensitised, the allergy is usually lifelong.
